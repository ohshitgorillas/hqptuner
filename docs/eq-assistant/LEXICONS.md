# LEXICONS.md — the validated attribute vocabularies

Companion to `SOURCES.md` (citations, disagreements), `vocabulary.json` (the term map the tuner actually loads), `TRANSDUCERS.md` (physical reality). Compiled 2026-07-25.

`vocabulary.json` maps a user's word to a filter. This document is the evidence base behind those words: four independent published vocabularies, what each actually defines, and — critically — **which of them assign frequencies and which do not.**

The single most important structural fact, and it governs how every entry in `vocabulary.json` should be read:

> **The rigorous vocabularies assign no frequencies. The vocabulary that assigns frequencies is not rigorous.**

ITU-R BS.2399 (the Sound Wheel), SAQI and Rumsey are elicited, validated, peer-reviewed or standards-body — and all three deliberately decline to attach Hz to a term. Holt's glossary, which is the closest match to how HQPTuner's users actually speak, assigns frequencies freely and cites nothing for any of them. Every Hz value in `vocabulary.json` therefore comes from a source weaker than the source that defines the *meaning* of the term it is attached to. That is not a flaw to hide; it is the honest structure of the field, and recording it is what keeps the map's confidence ratings meaningful.

**Verification legend** — as `SOURCES.md`, plus `[VA]` = read by a delegated research agent that returned verbatim quotes and a URL (same artefact class as `[V]`; the difference is who read it).

## 1. SAQI — Spatial Audio Quality Inventory `[VA]`

* **Citation.** Lindau, A.; Erbes, V.; Lepa, S.; Maempel, H.-J.; Brinkmann, F.; Weinzierl, S. (2014). "A Spatial Audio Quality Inventory for Virtual Acoustic Environments (SAQI." *Acta Acustica united with Acustica*, 100(5), 984–994. DOI: 10.3813/AAA.918778. Artefact transcribed: Lindau, A., *SAQI Test Manual*, Release 1.2 (12/03/2015), audio communication Group, TU Berlin. DOI: 10.14279/depositonce-1.
* **Access.** Open, no paywall, via TU Berlin DepositOnce.
* **Reliability.** Peer-reviewed, plus a primary research instrument published by the originating institution. **The most rigorously derived vocabulary in this document.**
* **Provenance of the vocabulary itself:** consensus vocabulary generated by a focus group of 21 German-speaking experts in virtual acoustics; five further experts verified unambiguity; eight bilingual experts verified the English translation. German and English versions are both normative.

**Frequency assignments: NONE.** The three band-specific timbre attributes (High-/Mid-/Low-frequency tone color) are each defined only as "Timbral change in a limited frequency range" — band edges deliberately unspecified. SAQI supports our *term → direction* mapping and contributes **zero** evidence for *term → Hz*.

48 attributes across 8 groups (Timbre, Tonalness, Geometry, Room, Time behavior, Dynamics, Artifacts, General). The entries that matter to HQPTuner, verbatim:

| Attribute | Circumscription (verbatim) | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| **Externalization** | "Describes the distinctness with which a sound source is perceived within or outside the head regardless of their distance. Terminologically often enclosed between the phenomena of in-head localization and out-of-head localization." | *more internalized – more externalized* |
| **Tone color bright-dark** | "Timbral impression which is determined by the ratio of high to low frequency components." | *darker – brighter* |
| **High-/Mid-/Low-frequency tone color** | "Timbral change in a limited frequency range." | *attenuated – emphasized* |
| **Sharpness** | "Timbral impression which e.g., is indicative for the force with which a sound source is excited… Emphasized high frequencies may promote a 'sharp' sound impression." | *less sharp – sharper* |
| **Comb filter coloration** | "Often perceived as tonal coloration. 'Hollow' sound. Example: speaking through a tube." | *less pronounced – more pronounced* |
| **Metallic tone color** | "Coloration with pronounced narrow-band resonances, often as a result of low density of natural frequencies." | *less pronounced – more pronounced* |
| **Roughness** | "Timbral impression of fierce or aggressive modulation/vibration, whereas individual oscillations are hardly distinguishable. Often rated as unpleasant." | *less rough – more rough* |
| **Width** | "Perceived extent of a sound source in horizontal direction." | *less wide – wider* |
| **Localizability** | "If localizability is low, spatial extent and location of a sound source are difficult to estimate, or appear diffuse… Low/high localizability is often associated with high/low perceived extent of a sound source." | *more difficult – easier* |
| **Crispness** | "Characteristic which is affected by the impulse fidelity of systems… Counter-example: Result of an equalization aiming at phase linearization." | *less pronounced – more pronounced* |
| **Clarity** | "Impression of how clearly different elements in a scene can be distinguished from each other…" | *less pronounced – more pronounced* |
| **Presence** | "Perception of 'being-in-the-scene', or 'spatial presence'." | *lower – higher* |

**Why this matters to the map.** *Externalization* is the validated term for "it's all inside my head" — a complaint our spatial vocabulary previously had no rigorous source for. *Comb filter coloration* is the validated term for "hollow". *Crispness* is explicitly tied to group-delay distortion and phase linearization, which is HQPlayer filter territory rather than EQ territory — a "smeared transients" complaint may not be an EQ complaint at all.

## 2. Rumsey 2002 — the scene-based paradigm for spatial attributes `[VA]`

* **Citation.** Rumsey, F. (2002). "Spatial Quality Evaluation for Reproduced Sound: Terminology, Meaning, and a Scene-Based Paradigm." *Journal of the Audio Engineering Society*, 50(9), 651–666.
* **Access.** Free full text at `ece.uvic.ca/~peterd/30604/rumseyattributes.pdf` (paywalled at the AES e-Library).
* **Reliability.** Peer-reviewed (JAES) — **with the author's own caveat**, which should travel with any citation: "It is presented as a contribution to the debate rather than a definitive account of completed work." Peer-reviewed proposal, not a ratified standard.

**Frequency assignments: NONE.** Purely spatial-construct definition and hierarchy.

The organising move: attributes are defined at distinct **scene levels** — individual source → ensemble → environment → scene — and split into **dimensional** attributes (width, depth, distance, height) versus **immersion** attributes (envelopment, presence).

**Width, the four levels** (Table 1, verbatim): *Individual source width* "Width of individual source(s) within a scene" · *Ensemble width* "Overall width of a defined group of sources" · *Environment width* "Broadness of (reflective) environment within which individual sources are located" · *Scene width* "Composite or global width of entire scene".

**Immersion** (Table 3, verbatim): *Individual / Ensemble / Environmental envelopment*, plus *Presence* — "Sense of being inside an (enclosed) space or scene".

**Miscellaneous** (Table 4, verbatim), two of which map directly to complaints we receive: *Source focus* "Degree to which individual sources can be precisely located in space" · *Scene left–right skew* "Degree to which a spatial audio scene is skewed to the left or right from a stated reference position" · plus *Source stability*, *Scene stability*, *Scene width homogeneity*.

**The relationship our "diffuse"/"unfocused" terms rest on**, verbatim:

> "When a source has a small ISW, it is also likely to have high locatedness… whereas when it has a large ISW, it is more likely to have poor locatedness… Listeners sometimes prefer to use terms such as poor image focus rather than large individual source width."

with an explicit hedge Rumsey states himself and which must not be dropped:

> "It is not clear, however, that a high source diffuseness is exactly congruent with large perceived width, or that these are identical attributes."

**Consequence for `vocabulary.json`:** our single `too wide` term flattens a four-level hierarchy. A user saying "too wide" may mean an individual instrument is smeared, or that the whole scene is over-spread — different complaints with different remedies. Rumsey also explicitly rejects "spatial impression" as a usable attribute ("not very helpful in practice, as it is not well defined"), and explicitly **defers** naturalness — do not cite Rumsey 2002 for a naturalness definition.

Also worth keeping, because it warns against over-precision in our own map:

> "at what point does the attribute we call source width become another one called envelopment? (The correct answer is probably, 'when subjects say that it does.')"

## 3. Holt — *Sounds Like? An Audio Glossary* `[VA]`

* **Citation.** Holt, J. Gordon. "Sounds Like? An Audio Glossary." *Stereophile*. Also published as *The Audio Glossary* (Audio Amateur Press, 1990, ISBN 0962419141).
* **Access note.** stereophile.com returns **HTTP 403** (Cloudflare) to both `curl` and the fetch tool. Text was transcribed from Internet Archive snapshots of the publisher's own pages, retrieved with `curl` and stripped locally. The artefact read is the publisher's page, served from archive rather than origin.
* **Reliability. PRACTITIONER / JOURNALISTIC.** Not peer-reviewed, not a standards body, not an elicited-and-validated vocabulary. One influential critic's codification of hi-fi review language. **Its authority is descriptive — it records how audiophiles actually talk, which is precisely why it matters to us — not empirical.**

~250 entries captured across all 9 letter pages, with **67 asserted-frequency flags**.

### 3.1 Every frequency in Holt is asserted

Nowhere in ~250 entries does Holt cite a measurement, an experiment, a listening panel, or any source for a number. There is no methodology. Two internal collisions demonstrate the point rather than merely suggesting it:

- **"ih" and "ee" are both given as 3.5 kHz** — they cannot both be right as distinct vowel colorations.
- **"presence range" (1–3 kHz) straddles his own treble boundary at 1300 Hz** and overlaps his own "lower highs" (1.3–2.6 kHz). He never reconciles them.

Any Hz taken from Holt must carry the `[FREQ — ASSERTED]` marker. The asserted-versus- justified distinction only holds if the flag travels with the number.

### 3.2 Holt's band map — and it is not the modern one

| Band name | Verbatim | Range |
|---|---|---|
| low bass | "The range from 20-40Hz." | 20–40 Hz |
| deep bass | "Frequencies below 40Hz." | <40 Hz |
| **midbass** | "The range of frequencies from 40-80Hz." | **40–80 Hz** |
| upper bass | "The range of frequencies from 80-160Hz." | 80–160 Hz |
| lower midrange | "The range of frequencies from 160-320Hz." | 160–320 Hz |
| midrange | "The range of frequencies from 160-1300Hz." | 160–1300 Hz |
| power range | "The frequency range about 200-500Hz that affects… the brass instruments." | 200–500 Hz |
| upper midrange | "The range of frequencies from 650-1300Hz." | 650–1300 Hz |
| treble | "The frequency range above 1.3kHz." | >1.3 kHz |
| lower highs | "The range of frequencies from 1.3-2.6kHz." | 1.3–2.6 kHz |
| presence range | "approximately 1-3kHz" | 1–3 kHz |
| middle highs | "The range of frequencies from 2.6-5kHz." | 2.6–5 kHz |
| top | "the range of audio frequencies above about 8kHz." | >8 kHz |
| extreme highs | "The range of audible frequencies above 10kHz." | >10 kHz |
| upper treble | "The range of frequencies from 10-20kHz." | 10–20 kHz |

**Holt's "midbass" is 40–80 Hz**, where modern usage puts it roughly 80–200 Hz. Since he defines **boomy** as "pronounced exaggeration of the **midbass**", following Holt strictly sends boomy to **40–80 Hz** — well below the 100–250 Hz contemporary charts use, and below our own 60–150 Hz ruling. A concrete, checkable disagreement originating in the *band vocabulary*, not in the complaint word.

### 3.3 The vowel ladder, and the terms it unlocks

Holt's most systematic and most internally coherent set of frequency claims. It roughly follows first-formant ordering, which is a point in its favour — but he cites no formant data.

| Vowel | Centre | Qualifier |
|---|---|---|
| "oo" (gloom) | 120 Hz | **broad** |
| "oh" (toe) | 250 Hz | **broad** |
| "aw" (paw) | 450 Hz | — |
| "ah" (rah) | 1000 Hz | — |
| "eh" (bed) | 2 kHz | — |
| "ee" (we) | 3.5 kHz | — |

He then defines several complaint words *by vowel*, so following his own cross-references yields numbers he never states directly:

| Term | Holt's clause | Resulting Hz |
|---|---|---|
| **nasal** | "Like the vowel 'eh' coloration." | **2 kHz** |
| **boxy** (sense 1) | "Characterized by an 'oh' vowel coloration…" | **250 Hz** |
| **honky** | "Pertaining to a severe 'aw' coloration." | **450 Hz** |
| **hooty** | "Pertaining to a severe 'ooo' coloration." | **120 Hz** |

**These are derived-by-cross-reference — Holt's links, our composition — and are a tier weaker than his direct claims.** He never writes "nasal = 2 kHz". Two further caveats: the hooty entry says `"ooo"` while the vowel entry is headed `"oo"` (almost certainly the same vowel, not a literal match); and **"oh" and "oo" are specified as broad peaks while "eh" and "ee" are not** — a Q hint straight from the source, which is otherwise rare.

**`nasal` also carries a shape claim our schema cannot currently express**: "often due to a measured peak in the upper midrange **followed by a complementary dip**" — a peak/dip pair, not a lone bell.

### 3.4 The one clean, testable band claim

> **bright, brilliant** — "Brightness relates to the energy content in the **4kHz-8kHz** band. **It is *not* related to output in the extreme-high-frequency range.**"

Unusually valuable despite the tier: a bounded band rather than a band *name*, carrying an **explicit negative claim**. It directly contradicts the common vendor-chart habit of mapping "bright" to a 10 kHz air shelf. If our map sends "too bright" to a high shelf at 10 kHz, Holt says we are treating the wrong band.

### 3.5 `warm` and `dark` are tilts, not bells — a filter-*type* disagreement

- **dark** — "The audible effect of a frequency response which is **clockwise-tilted across the entire range**, so that output diminishes with increasing frequency."
- **warm** — "The same as dark, but less tilted."
- **tilt** (his own formal definition) — "Across-the-board rotation of an otherwise flat frequency response… A linear frequency-response curve that is not horizontal."
- **light** (the mirror) — "Lean and tipped-up. The audible effect of a frequency response which is tilted **counterclockwise**."

So on Holt's own terms, `warm` is a **gentle broadband downward tilt**, explicitly not a bump in the lower midrange. If `vocabulary.json` renders warm as a peaking boost at 100–300 Hz, we are implementing the wrong filter *shape*, not merely the wrong centre frequency.

**Countervailing evidence within Holt himself — this is not one-sided:**

> **fat** — "The sonic effect of a moderate exaggeration of the mid- and upper-bass ranges. **Excessively 'warm.'**"

There he ties excess warmth to a mid/upper-bass exaggeration, which is the bump reading. Honest position: Holt's *formal mechanism* for warm/dark is a broadband tilt; his *informal usage* elsewhere is consistent with low-end lift. He does not reconcile them, and asserts both without method. **The disagreement is Holt-internal as well as Holt-versus-us.** See `SOURCES.md` §5 for the ruling.

### 3.6 Terms Holt does NOT give a band or mechanism for

`congested` · `harsh` ("Gratingly unpleasant to the ear" — no mechanism) · `sibilance` · `liquid` · `lush` · `presence` · `muddy` · `airy`.

**Do not cite Holt for a frequency on any of these**, whatever numbers circulate attributed to him. Note also that **his headword is `sibilance`, not `sibilant`** — there is no `sibilant` entry.

On sibilance specifically, the closest he comes is two neighbouring terms: **sizzly** ("Emphasis of the frequency range **above about 8kHz**, which adds sibilance to all sounds") and **spitty** ("a sharp response peak in the **upper treble range**", i.e. 10–20 kHz on his own map). Both sit *above* the 5–8 kHz where de-essers and most charts place sibilance. If our map uses ~6 kHz, Holt is not the source for it.

### 3.7 `veiled` is not purely a frequency claim

> **veiled** — "Pertaining to a deficiency of detail and focus, due to moderate amounts of distortion, treble-range restriction, or **attack rounding**."

Three alternative causes; only the middle one is an EQ move. **rounding** is defined separately as "The shearing-off of sharp attack transients, due to poor transient response or restricted HF range" — a time-domain fault. Mapping `veiled` to a treble shelf is defensible but partial by Holt's own wording, and the attack-rounding cause points at HQPlayer's filter selection rather than at EQ. Compare SAQI's **Crispness**, which ties the same percept to group-delay distortion.

## 4. Cross-source collisions

### 4.1 `presence` is a three-way homonym

| Source | Meaning | Band |
|---|---|---|
| **Holt** | "A quality of realism and aliveness", via *presence range* | 1–3 kHz (tonal) |
| **Rumsey 2002** | "Sense of being inside an (enclosed) space or scene" | none (spatial) |
| **SAQI** | "Perception of 'being-in-the-scene', or 'spatial presence'" | none (spatial) |

Rumsey and SAQI agree with each other and disagree with Holt. **A user typing "needs more presence" almost certainly means Holt's sense** — push the upper mids — while our two rigorous spatial citations define the word the other way. `vocabulary.json` must disambiguate this explicitly rather than silently picking one. Owsinski's "Presence" band (4–6 kHz, `SOURCES.md` §2.4) is a *fourth* reading, higher than Holt's.

### 4.2 `nasal` and `honky` may not be separate terms

Two practitioner sources (`SOURCES.md` §7) treat "nasal honk" as one phenomenon at 800 Hz–1.5 kHz. Holt separates them by vowel — nasal ("eh", 2 kHz) versus honky ("aw", 450 Hz) — nearly two octaves apart. **No source supports our current split at our current frequencies.** Either reading is defensible; the current pair is not obviously either.

### 4.3 The rigorous sources cannot adjudicate frequency disputes

ITU-R BS.2399, SAQI and Rumsey between them define the *meaning* and the *ordering* of most of our terms and the *frequency* of none. When `SOURCES.md` §4 rule 2 says "perceptual definition sets the kind, practitioner data sets the number", this is the concrete reason. There is no higher court for a Hz dispute than the practitioner literature, and the practitioner literature disagrees with itself (`SOURCES.md` §7).

## 5. What each lexicon is good for

| Source | Use it for | Do NOT use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **ITU-R BS.2399** (Sound Wheel) | Definitions; the low-bass/upper-bass *ordering* constraint | Any frequency |
| **SAQI** | Externalization, comb-filter/"hollow", crispness, spatial direction, validated scale ends | Any frequency |
| **Rumsey 2002** | The width/depth/envelopment hierarchy; source focus; scene skew | Any frequency; naturalness |
| **Holt** | How users actually phrase complaints; `bright` = 4–8 kHz; the warm/dark tilt reading; the vowel ladder | Any frequency he does not state; `harsh`, `muddy`, `congested`, `sibilance`, `airy`, `presence` |
| **Owsinski / practitioner charts** | Hz values, weighted by the consensus table in `SOURCES.md` §7 | Definitions; anything on a finished master without noting the mixing provenance |
