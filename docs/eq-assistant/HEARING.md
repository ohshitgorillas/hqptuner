# HEARING.md — the listener, not the transducer

Companion to `SOURCES.md` (citations), `PRIMER.md` (feature contract), `PSYCHOACOUSTICS.md` (audibility thresholds), `TRANSDUCERS.md` (what is being EQ-ed). Compiled 2026-07-26.

**Verification legend** — as `SOURCES.md`. `[VA]` throughout means a delegated research lane read the artifact and returned a verbatim quote plus a URL. Figures we computed from a quoted formula are labeled **derived** in place and are never presented as source claims. Numbers that surfaced only in search-engine summaries were **excluded, not demoted** — a summary is weaker than what `[S]` means in this project.

Four sources were read in full from local PDFs, all gitignored and not committed: Benjamin & Siedenburg (2023) (§5), the Frontiers 2026 review (§5.4), Jin et al. (2024) (§1.4), and ITU-T H.870 (§6.3). **Two of them cannot be read with `pdftotext`** — Jin et al. is a Caesar −1 shift across pages 2–8 *including digits*, so `6/18` decodes to `7029` and a naive extraction yields plausible wrong numbers; Benjamin & Siedenburg is offset by one glyph and loses minus signs. Both were read as rendered pages. This is recorded because those files are still in the working directory and the failure is silent rather than obvious.

## Why this document exists

Every other document in this base assumes the thing being corrected is a **transducer**, measured on a rig, deviating from a target curve. A large fraction of users are correcting something else. One put it exactly: *"I EQ to try to compensate for my ears, not to change the phones. If my hearing loss could be magicked away, I'm sure I'd be perfectly happy with my HD600, let alone HD800."*

For that user the headphone is incidental and the target curve is beside the point. The tuner cannot detect this, cannot measure it, and must not diagnose it — but it will be handed complaints generated by it, and it needs to know what is and is not reachable with four change types. **The honest finding of this document is that the reachable set is smaller than it looks, and that the most confident-sounding move — boost where the loss is — is the one the evidence most consistently contradicts.**

Nothing here licenses a refusal, a disabled control, or an idle gate. As with the physical causes in `TRANSDUCERS.md` §6, the correct response to an out-of-reach cause is a `clarify` — and, where the tuner can name something useful it cannot itself change, a `recommends` (D19).

## 1. The audiogram — what it is, and what it is not

### 1.1 dB HL is a normative scale, not a physical one

An audiogram is plotted in dB HL, which is defined by subtracting a frequency- and transducer-specific reference from dB SPL. `[VA]`

> "RETSPLs allow the results for a given transducer to be converted from decibel sound pressure level (dB SPL) measured on a standardized, calibrated ear simulator to the HL (dB HL). By design, the HL reported should be 0 dB HL for individuals with 'normal' hearing and allows results to be compared across audiometers and transducers. dB HL is, therefore, a normative scale."

Clavier et al. (2022), *JASA* 152(1):601, doi:10.1121/10.0012733 — peer-reviewed.

The offsets are large and strongly frequency-dependent. 0 dB HL corresponds to roughly 45 dB SPL at 125 Hz, 13.5 dB SPL at 500 Hz, 7.5 dB SPL at 1 kHz, 9 dB SPL at 2 kHz, and 15.5 dB SPL at 8 kHz. `[VA]` — Carl, Hohman & Cornejo, *StatPearls* NBK580531, textbook tier, **and the values are attributed there to the superseded ANSI S3.6-1996**; the current edition was not obtained (§10). Treat the shape as reliable and any single figure as indicative.

**Consequence, and it is the whole reason this section exists: a dB HL number cannot be pasted into an EQ curve.** The two scales differ by a frequency-dependent transform, and the prescription literature works in dB SPL at the eardrum via HL-to-SPL transforms, RETSPL and RECD (§4.2). A user who reports "30 dB of loss at 6 kHz" has not told the tuner how many dB of anything to add.

### 1.2 What gets tested, and why it stops at 8 kHz

A standard clinical audiogram covers 250 Hz to 8000 Hz at octaves and most inter-octaves, with 125 Hz and the extended high frequencies (9, 10, 11.2, 12.5, 14, 16, 20 kHz) available but not routine. `[VA]` — *StatPearls*, as above. The British Society of Audiology's recommended procedure tests 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000, 500, 250 Hz in that order, retests 1000 Hz, and adds 750/1500/3000/6000 Hz where needed — and explicitly excludes >8000 Hz from its scope. `[VA]`

> "The document does not cover high-frequency audiometry (>8000 Hz), screening audiometry, use of short-duration tone bursts, self-recording audiometry or sound-field audiometry."

British Society of Audiology (2018), *Recommended Procedure: Pure-tone air-conduction and bone-conduction threshold audiometry with and without masking* — professional-body.

The reason for the ceiling is **instrumental, not physiological**. `[VA]`

> "Audiometric testing in research and in clinical settings rarely considers frequencies above 8 kHz. However, the sensitivity of young healthy ears extends to 20 kHz […] EHF audiometry in the clinic presents with no particular difficulty, the biggest obstacle being lack of specialist equipment."

Lough & Plack (2022), *JASA* 151(3):1944, doi:10.1121/10.0009766 — peer-reviewed, abstract only.

**This is a useful coincidence worth carrying.** `TRANSDUCERS.md` §4 puts the measurement rig's qualified ceiling at 8 kHz with a 10 kHz confidence floor, and `PSYCHOACOUSTICS.md` §5 already treats corrections above 8 kHz as standing on undefined ground. The audiogram stops in the same place. Above 8 kHz the tuner has neither a trustworthy transducer measurement nor a trustworthy hearing measurement, and should say so rather than narrate confidently.

### 1.3 Grades, and the averaging that produces them

WHO's current grades, verbatim from the *World report on hearing* (2021), by threshold in the **better** ear: `[VA]`

| Grade | dB |
|---|---|
| Normal hearing | < 20 |
| Mild | 20 to < 35 |
| Moderate | 35 to < 50 |
| Moderately severe | 50 to < 65 |
| Severe | 65 to < 80 |
| Profound | 80 to < 95 |
| Complete / total | ≥ 95 |
| Unilateral | < 20 better ear, ≥ 35 worse ear |

The averaging rule is the load-bearing footnote:

> "'Hearing threshold' refers to the minimum sound intensity that an ear can detect as an average of values at 500, 1000, 2000, 4000 Hz in the better ear."

**A four-frequency average in the better ear discards exactly what matters to a music listener** — the shape of the loss, everything above 4 kHz, and the worse ear entirely. A "mild" grade is compatible with a steeply sloping high-frequency loss. WHO says so itself:

> "While audiometric descriptors (e.g. category, pure-tone average) provide a useful summary of an individual's hearing thresholds, they should not be used as the sole determinant in the assessment of disability or the provision of intervention(s) including hearing aids or cochlear implants."

> "The ability to detect pure tones using earphones in a quiet environment is not, in itself, a reliable indicator of hearing disability."

World Health Organization (2021), *World report on hearing*, ISBN 9789240020481 — standards/professional-body.

**Grade schemes disagree, so a self-reported grade is not a number.** ASHA's public materials use Clark (1981), which puts normal at −10 to 15 dB, adds a "slight" band at 16–25 dB, and starts mild at 26 dB. `[VA]` ASHA's own page concedes the disagreement: *"not all professionals agree on the cutoff for 'normal' hearing."* When a user says "mild loss", the tuner has learned less than it appears to have — and the fix is a `clarify`, not an assumption.

### 1.4 The spread is the finding

ISO 7029:2017 gives median threshold shift by age and sex as `ΔH_md = α_md · (Y − 18)^β_md`. The coefficients are reprinted in an open-access paper `[VA]` (De Raedemaeker et al. 2022, *Front Psychol* 13:861555), and from them the median shift relative to age 18 is **derived** below. **These are our arithmetic, not any source's.**

| Age | 1 kHz M/F | 2 kHz M/F | 4 kHz M/F | 8 kHz M/F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | 1.6 / 1.3 | 2.6 / 1.7 | 4.5 / 2.7 | 6.8 / 4.8 |
| 60 | 7.8 / 7.9 | 12.5 / 10.6 | 20.2 / 15.3 | 30.4 / 23.0 |
| 70 | 13.4 / 14.4 | 20.8 / 19.3 | 33.2 / 27.0 | 50.0 / 38.8 |
| 80 | 20.7 / 23.6 | 31.8 / 31.5 | 50.0 / 43.2 | 75.3 / 59.5 |

**Two corrections to that table, both load-bearing.**

First, **ISO 7029 itself deviates from real populations, and the deviation is sex-asymmetric and concentrated exactly where a music listener notices.** A 2024 review compared the standard against Czech, German, American and Korean datasets `[VA]`: *"the difference between the ISO 7029 data and the hearing threshold data for the four countries tended to be more pronounced for male adults than for female adults and for higher frequencies than for lower frequencies."* Where agreement breaks down is stated per sex and age group, never as a general boundary: for **males** in their 60s and 70s the four countries track ISO 7029 "below 2,000 Hz, but the higher-frequency regions above 3,000 Hz showed greater deviations", while for males in their 80s the boundary drops — similar below 1,000 Hz, greater deviation above 1,500 Hz. For **females** the paper locates the breakdown at 8,000 Hz specifically, agreement being within 5 dB below it. Concretely, for **males ISO underestimates**: at 8 kHz in the 60s *"the ISO 7029 data was 30 dB, while the Korean, German, and American data were 35, 41, and 45.3 dB"*; at 4 kHz in the 50s *"the deviation according to ISO 7029 was 11 dB, whereas the German data showed 15 dB; Czech data, 17.5 dB; Korean data, 19 dB; and American data, 20.4 dB."* For **females ISO overestimates** at 8 kHz: in the 50s *"the deviation value of ISO 7029 was 21 dB, whereas the four-country data showed a smaller variation of 5 dB (Czech) to 16 dB (Korea)."*

Jin et al. (2024), *J Audiol Otol* 28(1):1–9, doi:10.7874/jao.2023.00626 — peer-reviewed invited review, read from a local copy. **Preserve its own hedging:** it compares four countries with inconsistent reference ages and ear-selection rules, reports no significance testing, and explicitly declines a direct comparison in favour of *"trends in hearing threshold changes by frequency and age group."*

**So the table above is biased low for men at 4–8 kHz by roughly 5–15 dB in the ages most likely to be complaining, and biased high for women at 8 kHz.** That is a better caveat than "the coefficients may be superseded", because it has a direction and a magnitude.

Second, the **amendment question is open but bounded.** Jin et al. record that *"Currently, the amendment 'Correction of parameter values for estimating the hearing threshold distribution' is in progress to increase data accuracy"* — unpublished as of late 2023, and they print no corrected coefficients, so ours are neither confirmed nor superseded. The route to its substance is Kurakata (2023), the recalculation Jin's own comparison rests on (§10).

**The median is the least useful statistic here anyway.** What matters is the spread at a single age and frequency, and it is enormous. From NHANES 2011–2020, unscreened US adults, males aged 60–69 at 4000 Hz: 10th percentile **14.3** dB HL, 25th **21.3**, median **35.8**, 75th **53.2**, 90th **68.2**. At 8000 Hz the same group runs 20.7 / 33.0 / 53.5 / 70.0 / 82.4. `[VA]` — Humes (2023), *Trends in Hearing* 27, doi:10.1177/23312165231162727, peer-reviewed.

That is a **~54 dB spread between the 10th and 90th percentile at one age and one frequency.** Two listeners of the same age and sex can differ by more than the entire gain range the tuner is permitted to apply.

Progression, from a longitudinal cohort `[VA]` (Dillard et al. 2024, *Communications Medicine* 4:171, doi:10.1038/s43856-024-00593-w, peer-reviewed): 0.42 dB/year at 250 Hz rising monotonically to 0.98 at 4 kHz and 1.44 at 8 kHz; four-frequency average 0.83 dB/year. **Derived** ×10: roughly 9.8 dB/decade at 4 kHz and 14.4 dB/decade at 8 kHz. Prevalence rises from 15.4 % of people in their 60s to 58.2 % above 90 for moderate-or-worse loss (WHO, above).

**Operational consequence.** Age tells the tuner almost nothing about an individual, and the tuner must never infer a curve from an age, a grade, or a self-description. What the numbers license is a prior — that a listener over 60 complaining about lost detail in the top two octaves is describing something plausible and physical — not a correction.

## 2. A threshold shift is not a gain figure

This is the single most important section in the document, because the naive move is arithmetically wrong in a way that sounds right.

### 2.1 Mirroring the audiogram was tried, and wearers rejected it

The theoretical case for gain equal to loss holds only *at threshold*, and it does not survive contact with listeners. `[VA]`

> "Consequently, 40 dB of functional gain must be provided to someone with a 40-dB hearing loss and 80 dB to someone with a 80-dB hearing loss so that both can hear a sound at 0 dB HL as would a listener with normal hearing. […] **Gain of the hearing aid should decrease for input levels above 0 dB HL to compensate for recruitment.** Although theoretically reasonable, having an aided threshold at 0 dB HL may not be feasible or practical. **This would require the hearing aid gain to equal the wearer's hearing loss. Clinical experience suggests that mirroring of the audiogram has not been acceptable to wearers of linear hearing aids, who accepted substantially less gain than the mirrored gain.**"

Kuk & Ludvigsen (2003), *Trends in Amplification* 7(3):77–97, PMCID PMC4168919 — peer-reviewed. Emphasis in the original lane report.

Note what this quote contains: the mirrored-gain calculation, the reason it fails (recruitment), and the empirical verdict (wearers took less), in one passage. It is the cleanest single citation in the whole base for the tuner's central caution.

### 2.2 The half-gain rule, and why it is half

The historical quantitative answer is Lybarger's half-gain rule — prescribed insertion gain ≈ half the threshold shift. `[VA]`

> "A one-half gain rule for hearing aid fitting based on the unaided average hearing threshold level was first proposed by S. F. Lybarger. […] The one-half gain rule was found to be valid for speech reception threshold except with mild hearing losses where the gain obtained was a little less than one-half the unaided hearing threshold level. The one-half gain rule is easily computed and provides a good first-order approximation of operating gain needs."

Berger, Hagberg & Rane (1980), *Ear Hear* 1(4):223–225, PMID 7409361 — peer-reviewed, abstract.

**The mechanism is that most-comfortable loudness rises more slowly than threshold.** `[VA]`

> "two hearing-impaired listeners, each having a hearing threshold of 50 dB HL at 1000 Hz, may have most comfortable loudness levels that are elevated 15 and 35 dB (on average, 25 dB) above the normal value"

Humes et al. (1996), *Trends in Amplification* 1(4):121–135, doi:10.1177/108471389600100402 — peer-reviewed.

**Derived:** 25 dB mean MCL elevation ÷ 50 dB threshold elevation = 0.5, which is the half-gain coefficient. **The source does not draw this inference; do not attribute it to them.**

**And the same sentence destroys the coefficient's usefulness for an individual.** Two listeners with *identical* 50 dB HL thresholds at one frequency had MCLs elevated 15 and 35 dB. Half-gain is a population mean with a ±10 dB individual spread at a single frequency. The same paper says so directly: *"it would be difficult to accurately predict loudness judgments from thresholds."*

**Derived, trivially:** a 30 dB threshold shift at 6 kHz implies roughly 15 dB of prescribed insertion gain, not 30 — and even that figure carries the ±10 dB spread above, plus the dB HL → dB SPL problem from §1.1.

### 2.3 Three independent literatures converge

This is the strongest claim in the document, because nothing in it depends on a single study.

1. **Linear hearing-aid wearers** accepted substantially less than mirrored gain (Kuk & Ludvigsen, §2.1).
2. **Self-fitting users** given a simple interface chose gain averaging 1.8 dB below the audiologist's overall and 5.6 dB per band, and *"the user-selected gains were slightly lower than those selected by the audiologist."* `[VA]` — Sabin et al. (2020), *Trends in Hearing* 24, doi:10.1177/2331216519900589.
3. **Self-fitted OTC devices in the field** undershoot NAL-NL2 targets, and the undershoot is **worst exactly where age-related loss lives**: correspondence to the 5 dB criterion was 75.9 % at 250 Hz and 52.4 % at 500 Hz, but 17.1 % at 4000 Hz and 24.1 % at 6000 Hz. *"SF-OTC hearing aids generally provide user-selected gain levels lower than clinical targets, particularly at higher frequencies."* `[VA]` — Knoetze et al. (2025), *Audiol Res* 15(1):17, doi:10.3390/audiolres15010017.

**The rule this supports.** When a user asks the tuner to compensate for high-frequency loss, the correct starting magnitude is **well below** the threshold shift, applied broadly, and revised by ear. A tuner that reasons "you have 30 dB of loss at 6 kHz, so here is +30 dB, or even +15 dB" is reproducing a fitting that three separate literatures say people reject. Start small, let the listener drive, and treat their "that's too much" as data rather than as a correction to be argued with — which is also what `PRIMER.md`'s prefer-additive-fills rule already says for a different reason.

## 3. What restoring audibility does not restore

EQ can only change level as a function of frequency. Cochlear loss is not only a level deficit, and the residual deficits are the reason "I made it audible" and "I made it sound right" are different achievements.

### 3.1 Recruitment, and the compressed dynamic range

Loss of outer-hair-cell compression raises threshold while leaving the discomfort ceiling roughly where it was, so the usable range shrinks from both ends of the argument. Measured with categorical loudness scaling `[VA]`:

> "For normal-hearing subjects, SPL varied by 75–80 dB, whereas for the subjects with moderate hearing loss, SPL varied by only 20–40 dB for the same loudness range."

> "the soft slopes for the subjects with the greatest amount of hearing loss in the current study are about 3 times steeper."

Al-Salim et al. (2010), *Ear and Hearing* 31(4):567–578, doi:10.1097/AUD.0b013e3181da4d15 — peer-reviewed.

In severe-to-profound loss the range can close almost entirely `[VA]`: *"The range from the threshold to the loudness discomfort level can be as small as 5 dB at some frequencies. Because conversational speech varies over 30 dB SPL or more, it is difficult to place the full range of speech components into the audible range of the listener using only linear amplification."* — Souza (2002), *Trends in Amplification* 6(4):131–65.

**Keep recruitment and hyperacusis separate; conflating them is a common error.** In recruitment the uncomfortable level is *"essentially unchanged"* while loudness grows abnormally fast; in hyperacusis the ceiling itself drops, from ~100 dB HL to 70–80 dB HL. `[VA]` — Shi et al. (2022), *Med Sci Monit* 28:e936373. The reduced-headroom argument belongs mostly to the second case (§7.3).

**Honest counter-evidence.** Loudness growth in impaired ears is not uniformly rapid `[VA]`: *"individual loudness-growth functions encompass a wide range of shapes from rapid growth to softness imperception."* — Marozeau & Florentine (2007), *JASA* 122(3):EL81, abstract only. Do not assert that every impaired listener has a steeply compressed range.

**Consequence for the tuner.** A static boost that makes a quiet passage right will overshoot a loud one, because the correction the impaired ear needs is level-dependent and the tuner's is not. This is a real limitation to state, not to engineer around — HQPTuner's chain has no compressor and the tuner must not imply otherwise.

### 3.2 Broadened auditory filters — and what this does to the ERB argument

Measured ERBs, normal-hearing versus hearing-impaired listeners `[VA]`:

> "average ERBs for the NH group at the five probe frequencies were 68, 150, 298, 454, and 590 Hz, respectively."

> "On average, ERBs at 250, 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz for these 43 conditions were 142, 145, 359, 658, and 2434 Hz, respectively, for the HI listeners"

Desloge et al. (2012), *Trends in Amplification* 16(1):19–39, doi:10.1177/1084713812445510 — peer-reviewed.

**Derived** broadening factors (HI ÷ NH, our arithmetic, not the paper's): 2.09× at 250 Hz, 0.97× at 500 Hz, 1.20× at 1 kHz, 1.45× at 2 kHz, **4.13× at 4 kHz**. Two cautions that must travel with these numbers: the groups were different listeners, so these are between-group ratios; and the paper's own "factor of 1.3" is impaired-versus-*simulated* loss, which is easy to misquote. The clean within-listener evidence is Glasberg & Moore's unilateral design, whose body numbers were not obtained (§10) — its abstract confirms the direction: *"the equivalent rectangular bandwidth of the filter was always greater for the impaired ear than for the normal ear at each center frequency."* `[VA]`

**This changes the standing of `PSYCHOACOUSTICS.md` §1 and must be recorded there as well as here.** That section derives the low-Q preference from normal-hearing ERBs — ~133 Hz at 1 kHz, so Q ≈ 7.5 is about one ERB. For an impaired listener the filters are wider, so **the conclusion strengthens while the derivation no longer applies.** A narrow filter is even further inside a single auditory filter than the normal-hearing arithmetic suggests. The practical rule is unchanged and better supported: broad moves for voicing.

Also from the same abstract, and load-bearing for spread of masking: *"For most subjects, the lower branch of the filter was much less steep than normal."* That asymmetric broadening is the mechanism by which low-frequency energy masks higher-frequency detail more effectively in an impaired ear — which is a legitimate primary-source route to the upward-spread-of-masking claim, whose own numeric sources stayed blocked (§10).

### 3.3 Dead regions — the one hard bound on *where* to boost

This is the most actionable material in the document, and it is free and verbatim. `[VA]`

> "When the IHCs are non-functioning over a certain region of the cochlea, no transduction will occur in that region. For this reason, I will refer to such a region as a *dead region*."

> "Amplification of frequencies well inside a high-frequency dead region usually does not improve speech intelligibility, and may sometimes impair it."

> "However, there may be some benefit in amplifying frequencies up to 50 to 100% above the estimated low-frequency edge of a high-frequency dead region."

> "For people with moderate-to-severe high-frequency hearing loss, amplification of the high frequencies in speech is not always beneficial, and may sometimes be harmful."

Moore (2001), *Trends in Amplification* 5(1):1–34, doi:10.1177/108471380100500102 — peer-reviewed, free at PMC4168936.

Prevalence is not marginal: roughly a quarter to a third of hearing-aid candidates test positive at one or more frequencies. `[VA]` — *"31% of these individuals were found to test positive for a DR at one or more test frequencies"*; *"Of the 307 ears tested, 70 (23%) were found to have a DR"* — Cox et al. (2011), *Ear Hear* 32(3):339–348.

**And the clinical recommendation is contested, which must be stated.** The same paper: *"This study did not produce support for a recommendation to reduce high-frequency hearing aid gain for amplification candidates with moderate to severe hearing loss who test positive for DRs"*, and *"there is no evidence in this or any of the reviewed published investigations that the predicted problems actually occur in listeners with moderate to severe hearing loss."*

**The rule that survives either reading** — and it is a genuinely useful one the tuner can act on — is **boost near the low-frequency edge of a loss region, not deep inside it.** Moore's "up to 50 to 100 % above the edge" is a concrete span. Where a user reports a steeply sloping high-frequency loss, extending a shelf far past the edge spends gain and headroom on a region that may return nothing, while a move anchored near the edge is defensible under both Moore's and Cox's readings.

### 3.4 Temporal fine structure, and diplacusis

Reduced sensitivity to temporal fine structure is documented and is not a level deficit `[VA]`: *"Moderate cochlear hearing loss results in a reduced ability, or no ability, to discriminate harmonic from frequency-shifted tones based on TFS"*, and *"for most subjects and F0s, performance was not significantly above chance."* — Moore (2008), *JARO* 9(4):399–406, doi:10.1007/s10162-008-0143-x. **That paper says nothing about whether amplification restores TFS use; do not imply it does.**

Binaural diplacusis — the same tone heard at a different pitch in each ear — is common in impaired listeners and larger than most people expect. `[VA]`

> "Statistically significant pitch differences between the left and right ears were observed in normal-hearing participants, but the effect was usually small (less than 1.5/16 octave, or about 7%). For the hearing-impaired participants, statistically significant interaural pitch differences were found in about three-quarters of the cases. Moreover, for about half of these participants, the difference exceeded 1.5/16 octaves and, in some participants, was as large as or larger than 1/4 octave."

> "For the vast majority of the hearing-impaired participants, the direction of the pitch differences was such that pitch was perceived as higher on the side with the higher (i.e., 'worse') hearing thresholds"

Colin et al. (2016), *PLOS ONE* 11(8):e0159975, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0159975 — peer-reviewed. **Cite the abstract only**; the fetched body text returned figures that contradict it and are internally inconsistent.

The load-bearing number is that threshold asymmetry accounted for **only ~22 % of the variance** in pitch mismatch. **Two consequences, and the second is ours as an inference, flagged as such:** a per-ear gain correction built from per-ear thresholds would not address most of the effect; and the tuner's four change types are **stereo-symmetric anyway**, so interaural pitch differences are structurally out of reach. Chasing an asymmetric percept with a symmetric filter mistunes both ears. Diplacusis is also a pitch phenomenon rather than a gain error, so even a hypothetical per-ear EQ would not correct it.

## 4. Why static EQ is not a prescription

The gap is dimensional, not a matter of quality or resolution.

### 4.1 Prescriptions are level-dependent by construction

NAL-NL2 was not derived at one input level. `[VA]`

> "The adaptive process was used to derive the optimal gain-frequency responses for 240 audiograms, covering a wide range of severity and slopes, each at seven speech input levels from 40 to 100 dB SPL."

> "The optimised gain values from all the audiograms and all the input levels were drawn together into a single composite prescription formula."

> "Hearing aid users with mild or moderate loss preferred a relatively higher gain reduction for higher input levels (80 dB SPL) but a relatively smaller gain reduction for lower input levels (50 dB SPL)."

Keidser et al. (2011), *Audiol Res* 1(1):e24, PMID 26557309 — peer-reviewed, open access.

DSL v5 is likewise specified across a level range and was built on loudness normalization `[VA]`: *"the range of input levels that receive primary prescriptive consideration now extends from 52 to 74 dB SPL"*, and *"The algorithm computed a narrow-band, curvilinear, target input-output function that would amplify all input levels to a normal loudness."* — Scollie et al. (2005), *Trends in Amplification* 9(4):159–197, doi:10.1177/108471380500900403 (obtained as a manufacturer-hosted reprint of the peer-reviewed article; label it that way).

**A single static EQ curve is one slice through a level-dependent surface.** It can be correct at one playback level and wrong at another, and nothing in the chain adapts. Souza (2002) states the resulting bind directly `[VA]`: *"the benefit of a linear hearing aid is restricted when the amplification needed to make low-intensity sounds audible amplifies high-intensity sounds to the point of discomfort."*

### 4.2 Insertion gain is not EQ gain

Prescribed gain is real-ear **insertion** gain, defined by subtracting the open ear's own response. `[VA]`

> "REIG is derived by subtracting the ear's natural amplification (Real Ear Unaided Gain, REUG) from the aided gain (Real Ear Aided Gain, REAG). REIG = REAG – REUG."

> "However, REIG does not provide any indication of dynamic range or signal audibility"

British Society of Audiology / British Academy of Audiology (2018), *Guidance on the verification of hearing devices using probe microphone measurements* (REMS-2018) — professional body.

Converting between a coupler and a real ear requires the RECD, which is individual: *"Once the amount by which the coupler differs from the real ear is known, this can be applied to all other measurements made in the coupler."* When individual data are absent, DSL substitutes an **average adult RECD** `[VA]` — i.e. the generic-transfer assumption is explicit in the prescription's own documentation. Its numeric inter-subject spread was not obtained (§10).

For headphones specifically, individual geometry dominates at exactly the frequencies in question `[VA]`: *"The individual differences are caused by individually distributed centre frequencies of dips and notches due to individual geometries"*, with the literature converging on *"higher variations (up to 10–15 dB) for frequencies above 10 kHz."* — Kohnen et al. (2021), *Acta Acustica* 5:58, doi:10.1051/aacus/2021051, open access. **The DT770/ER4/HD650 per-device figures in that paper are repositioning and cross-site repeatability bounds, not inter-subject spread — do not present them as the latter.**

### 4.3 The three things the tuner structurally cannot do

* **No compression.** The prescription is level-dependent; the chain is not (§4.1).
* **No per-ear control.** All four change types are stereo-symmetric. Asymmetric loss and diplacusis are out of reach (§3.4, §8).
* **No calibrated reference.** The tuner does not know the playback level in dB SPL at the eardrum, which is the unit every prescription is specified in (§1.1, §4.2).

None of these is a defect to apologize for; all three are boundaries to state plainly when a user's request crosses them. Crossing one is a `clarify`, and often a `recommends` — a user who needs level-dependent gain needs a hearing aid, and saying so once is more use than an EQ curve that pretends.

## 5. What impaired listeners actually asked for

**Read the whole section before quoting any of it.** The key study's abstract and its conclusion point in opposite directions, and the abstract is the misleading one.

The study is Benjamin & Siedenburg (2023), *JASA* 154(2):1048–1061, doi:10.1121/10.0020269 `[VA]` — read in full from a local copy. It gave listeners three controls over popular multitrack music and let them set each by ear:

* **LAR** — lead-to-accompaniment ratio, in dB. Requires stems. **Out of the tuner's reach.**
* **SPBal** — a spectral tilt in dB/octave between 125 Hz and 8 kHz, pivoting at 1 kHz. Crucially, *"The filter is applied on the final stereo mix, which encompasses all tracks"* (p. 1051), so **this one is exactly a playback-chain EQ move and its results do transfer to us.**
* **EQ-transform** — a per-track spectral-contrast/sparsity control that extrapolates each track's spectrum away from an average-instrument reference. Requires stems. **Structurally unreachable**, so its results are irrelevant to the tuner in either direction.

### 5.1 The abstract's null is narrower than it looks

The abstract says HI participants *"did not suggest distinct preferences regarding spectral balancing or EQ-transform."* That statement is scoped to **Experiment 1's between-group comparison only** — and the paper's own Conclusion (§VI, p. 1060) says:

> "In addition to suggesting that previous findings on LAR preferences of CI listeners extend toward HA users, it was also shown that there are distinct preferences regarding the setting of spectral mixing effects."

**Do not cite this paper for "impaired listeners have no distinct spectral preference."** The between-group null on SPBal is `F(2,420) = 1.13, p = 0.32` on ten listeners per impaired group, with the normal-hearing group averaging 28 years old against roughly 67–70 for the impaired groups. The authors flag the aid-use confound themselves and never test the age difference at all, while reporting age against loss at `r(43) = 0.9, p < 0.001`. It is an underpowered, age-confounded, explicitly exploratory null.

### 5.2 What the study actually found about tilt — and it is a positive result

Split by hearing-aid use, the tilt preferences separate cleanly (§III B, p. 1056):

| Group | Preferred SPBal | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Normal-hearing | +0.2 dB/Oct (SD 0.8) | `t(24) = 1.3, p = 0.2` — not significant |
| Impaired, **wearing** aids | +0.1 dB/Oct (SD 0.6) | `t(9) = 0.6, p = 0.6` — not significant |
| Impaired, **not** wearing aids | **+0.6 dB/Oct** (SD 0.7) | `t(9) = 2.4, p = 0.04, d = 0.77` |

Experiment 2 tested the same listeners aided and unaided, which removes the between-group confound, and it is the best-supported spectral result in the paper: `−0.74 dB/Oct` aided versus `+0.64 dB/Oct` unaided, `t(17) = 3.62, p = 0.002, d = 0.9` (large effect). The authors restate it as *"A −0.7 dB/Oct balance favoring low frequency with HA use and a similar positive balance of around +0.7 dB/Oct favoring high frequencies with no HA use."*

**This matters to HQPTuner specifically because its users are, overwhelmingly, listening unaided through headphones.** The aided condition is the one with no distinct preference; the unaided condition is the one that wanted a rising tilt.

**And the magnitude is the point.** +0.64 dB/Oct across 125 Hz–8 kHz is about six octaves, so roughly **4 dB end to end** — **derived**, our arithmetic from the printed dB/Oct figure. That is a gentle tilt, not a large treble boost, and it lands in the same territory as §2.3: the sourced move is small. For calibration, the authors note prior work found *"rather steep psychometric functions of spectral balance in the range of −2 to 2 dB"*, so a few dB of tilt is perceptually consequential rather than negligible.

Across the pooled sample, preference for a rising tilt tracked hearing loss only **marginally** — `r(51) = 0.3, p = 0.06` — against `r = 0.7, p < 0.001` for LAR and `r = 0.5, p < 0.001` for the EQ-transform. So loss predicts wanting the vocal louder far better than it predicts wanting more treble.

### 5.3 The caveat that keeps this honest

The authors read the unaided tilt as **compensating for what the aid would otherwise supply**, not as an aid-independent preferred curve (p. 1059):

> "A reduced spectral balance preference among such listeners as shown here may highlight the fact that they may counter the compensation of high frequency hearing loss brought on by the hearing aids."

So the defensible claim is narrow and worth stating exactly: **an unaided listener with mild-to-moderate loss asking for a gentle rising tilt — on the order of half a dB per octave, a few dB overall — is asking for something listeners in their position chose by ear in a controlled experiment.** That is not a target curve, it is not derivable from an audiogram, and it does not license a large treble boost. It is a starting magnitude with evidence behind it.

**What remains unsupported:** any *distinctive hearing-loss target curve*, and any peer-reviewed demonstration that consumer audiogram-derived EQ improves music listening. The authors' own follow-up found impaired listeners underperformed *"irrespective of the spectral manipulations"* with *"no main effects of EQ-transform"* `[VA]` — Benjamin & Siedenburg (2025), *PLOS ONE* 20(1):e0316442, which confounds loss with age (71 versus 26.6).

### 5.4 The review-level view

A 2026 review reaches the same place from above `[VA]`: *"Hearing loss significantly diminishes music perception and enjoyment, and current hearing devices can only partially restore these qualities"*, and the reason — *"Hearing devices have been optimized for speech perception rather than the experience of music, leaving limited tools to improve music outcomes."* It corroborates the LAR finding independently, citing Benjamin & Siedenburg for *"a preference for sound mixes with more prominent lead vocals in HA vs. NH users"*, and supports how thin the ground is: on music training for hearing-aid users, *"Has not been explored to date."*

Lalwani et al. (2026), *Frontiers in Audiology and Otology*, doi:10.3389/fauot.2026.1748554 — peer-reviewed narrative review, read in full from a local copy. **It is a narrative review restating cited primaries; attribute its claims to the sources it names.** It does not discuss EQ, target curves, dead regions or recruitment — do not cite it for any of those.

**One framing from it that the tuner should sit with**, because it describes the tuner's own mechanism as a hazard: *"Linear distortions are fixed frequency amplifications, which can change the harmonic spectrum, or natural timbre, of instruments."* Fixed frequency amplification is what HQPTuner does. The same review records that unaided listeners describe music as *"muddy," "muffled," and "distant"*, and that with hearing aids the descriptors shift to *"harsh"* and *"tinny"*, with some finding it *"too bright or shrill."* High-frequency compensation overshoots easily, which is the third independent line arriving at §2.3's conclusion.

**A refinement, not a contradiction:** the review cites Looi et al. (2019) for impaired listeners preferring *"music with male singers and lower pitched instruments, with the preferences being stronger for more severe HL."* That is a preference in *program material*, offered as a hypothesis about high-frequency-biased loss — not a shaping prescription. It is worth knowing that "no distinct spectral preference" was never the same claim as "no spectral preferences of any kind."

### 5.5 Self-fitting, on its own terms

Self-fitting outcomes are genuinely encouraging `[VA]` — an OTC self-fitting device matched audiologist-fitted best practice at six weeks (*"the differences were not meaningful on any subscale or global benefit scores between the groups"*) and at eight months — De Sousa et al. (2023), *JAMA Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 149(6):522–530, and (2024) 150(9):765–771. **But those trials measured speech and self-report, not music, and enforced a severity ceiling in their inclusion criteria.** They support "a layperson can usefully adjust their own amplification"; they do not support "EQ fixes music for hearing loss".

## 6. Ceilings — where more gain stops paying, and starts costing

### 6.1 The audibility ceiling is threshold-dependent, not a frequency

No source found names a frequency above which high-frequency gain universally stops helping, and the tuner must not imply one exists. What exists is a threshold criterion `[VA]`:

> "There was a clear pattern in the results suggesting that as the degree of hearing loss at a given frequency increased beyond 55 dB HL, the efficacy of providing additional audibility to that frequency region was diminished, especially when this degree of hearing loss was present at frequencies of 4000 Hz and above."

> "In some cases for more severely impaired listeners, increasing the audibility of high-frequency speech information resulted in no further improvement in speech recognition, or even decreases in speech recognition."

Hogan & Turner (1998), *JASA* 104(1):432–41, PMID 9670535 — peer-reviewed, abstract.

NAL's own prescriptive conclusion is blunter `[VA]`: *"for people with severe or profound losses at the high frequencies, amplification should only achieve a low or zero sensation level at this region, contrary to the implications of the unmodified SII."* — Ching, Dillon & Byrne (1998), *JASA* 103(2):1128–40. The mechanism is §3.3's dead regions; the contested status is Cox et al.'s.

### 6.2 A regulator's view of how peaky a compensatory response may be

The FDA's OTC hearing aid rule sets output and response limits that are worth knowing because they are the only place a regulator has quantified this. `[VA]`

> "No single peak in the one-third-octave frequency response shall exceed 12 dB relative to the average levels of the one-third-octave bands, two-thirds octave above and below the peak."

> "An OTC hearing aid shall not exceed an output limit of 111 dB SPL at any frequency except as provided in paragraph (d)(2) of this section."

21 CFR 800.30(d)–(e) — regulatory primary. The compression-activated ceiling is 117 dB SPL, total harmonic distortion plus noise ≤ 5 %, and the FDA's own framing is that 117 dB SPL *"is intended to maximize the available headroom […] while still providing reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness."*

**The 12 dB one-third-octave peak limit is a useful external corroboration**, landing in the same numeric territory as AutoEq's ±12 dB hard clamp already recorded in `PRIMER.md`'s guardrail table. It is not a constraint on HQPTuner — it governs a different class of device — but a compensatory peak beyond that is outside what a regulator considers reasonable for a device whose whole purpose is compensating for loss.

**A limit that does *not* transfer:** hearing-aid maximum stable gain at 2–4 kHz is 25–35 dB, bounded by the acoustic feedback loop between receiver and microphone `[VA]` (Marcrum et al. 2018, *Int J Audiol* 57(10):737–745, abstract). A closed headphone has no microphone in that loop, so this ceiling is absent — the headphone's limits are transducer headroom, digital headroom and §6.1's audibility ceiling. Do not cite feedback limits at a headphone user.

### 6.3 Compensatory gain is an exposure increase

**This is the ethically load-bearing item in the document.** A user boosting a region to compensate for loss is raising their exposure, and the mechanism that damaged their hearing in the first place is dose.

ITU-T Recommendation H.870 (03/2022), *Guidelines for safe listening devices/systems* — the WHO–ITU standard, read in full from a local copy `[VA]`, standards-body. It defines the reference dose as **1.6 Pa²h per 7 days** for adults, derived from *"80 dB(A) for a maximum of 40 hours per week"* (§7.1), and **0.51 Pa²h** for the more conservative mode, from 75 dBA / 40 h.

**The exchange rate is 3 dB**, stated as part of the metric's definition (§3.2.11): *"calculated using a 3-dB exchange rate between level and time"*, with §3.2.14 defining the term as *"The change in average noise level (in dB) that corresponds to a doubling or halving of allowable exposure time."* §8.1.3 makes it testable behavior: *"verify that the dose grows at twice the rate for every 3 dB increase in output level."* The 5 dB rule found in some occupational regulation appears nowhere in H.870.

The standard **prints the trade-off**, so nothing needs deriving. Table 1, Mode 1 (adults, 1.6 Pa²h/week), verbatim:

| dB(A) SPL | Weekly listening time |
|---|---|
| 80 | 40 h |
| 83 | 20 h |
| 86 | 10 h |
| 89 | 5 h |
| 92 | 2.5 h |
| 95 | 75 min |
| 98 | 37.5 min |
| 101 | 19 min |
| 104 | 9.5 min |
| 107 | 4.5 min |

So **+6 dB quarters the permitted week** — 40 hours becomes 10.

**The error this section exists to prevent.** H.870's dose is a **single broadband A-weighted quantity**. There is no per-band dose and no per-band limit anywhere in the standard. **The table above therefore may not be applied to one band's gain.** A narrow peaking filter lifted 6 dB raises the broadband A-weighted level by far less than 6 dB, so "+6 dB on a treble band quarters your listening time" is **wrong**. The honest statement concerns broadband level, and a narrowband boost costs proportionally less — how much less depends on the filter and the program material, which the tuner would have to actually compute rather than assert.

**What an uncalibrated tool is licensed to say**, and this is the standard's own ruling on the matter (§7.2). H.870 lists the uncertainty sources — unknown headphone type, artificial-versus-real ears, standardized diffuse-field correction versus individual HRTFs, fit, individual susceptibility, exposure from other sources — and concludes that *"an error of 3 dB constitutes a 100% dose error"* so *"uncertainties of dose estimation can be expected to be hundreds of percent."* Its instruction:

> "It is therefore suggested to refrain from signalling 'safe' and 'green' to the user based on dose readings below a certain limit."

> "The dose estimation is however relevant in accounting for the general trends: – higher signal level means higher risk – longer exposure means higher risk – the spectral content of the music is accounted for."

**Those three trends are the whole of what HQPTuner may claim**, since it has no calibrated reference at all (§4.3) — H.870 assumes level is measured at the eardrum reference point on a head-and-torso simulator with diffuse-field correction, and where the headphone is unknown a compliant device must assume *"the maximum permitted headphone sensitivity."* The tuner knows none of this. It may say a boost increases risk; it may not quantify the risk, and it must never tell a user a setting is safe.

**Two scope limits.** H.870 excludes hearing aids and personal sound amplification products from its scope entirely (§1), and makes no allowance adjustment for pre-existing hearing loss — individual susceptibility appears only as an uncertainty source. So the standard does not actually cover the compensating-for-loss case, and the tuner must not imply that it blesses or forbids it.

**And the counterpoint, which belongs here rather than being quietly omitted.** H.870 lists among desirable features *"Personalised processing to improve clarity of content, making lower levels more acceptable."* EQ that genuinely improves clarity can let someone listen *quieter*, which reduces dose. The exposure argument is not one-directional, and a tuner that only ever warns is misrepresenting the standard it is citing.

For occupational context, H.870's own Appendix VI records 85 dBA over an 8-hour day and 40-hour week as 5.06 Pa²h with *"8% at risk for 'material hearing impairment'"*, and 90 dBA as 16 Pa²h with 25% at risk — while noting that *"Single-number level limiting ignores well-established science."*

The right handling is a single factual note when a user asks for a large broadband boost, not a nag on every turn, and never a refusal.

## 7. Tinnitus, hyperacusis, and the notching question

### 7.1 Prevalence and pitch

Tinnitus affects 14.4 % (95 % CI 12.6–16.5) of adults and 23.6 % (19.4–28.5) of those aged 65 and over; severe tinnitus 2.3 %. `[VA]` — Jarach et al. (2022), *JAMA Neurol* 79(9):888–900, doi:10.1001/jamaneurol.2022.2189, abstract.

Pitch tracks the region of maximum loss rather than the audiogram's edge `[VA]`: *"The study-results confirm a relationship between tinnitus pitch and maximum hearing loss but not to the edge frequency, suggesting that tinnitus is rather a fill-in-phenomenon."* — Schecklmann et al. (2012), *PLOS ONE* 7(4):e34878.

### 7.2 Notched EQ is not supported, and the tuner must not offer it

The intervention in the literature is **tailor-made notched music training** (TMNMT): a one-octave notch centered on the tinnitus frequency, listened to for roughly 12 hours a week over months. The founding study was positive with **eight participants per arm** `[VA]` (Okamoto et al. 2010, *PNAS* 107(3):1207–1210), and a follow-up found the effect only for tinnitus frequencies ≤ 8 kHz `[VA]` (Teismann et al. 2011, *PLOS ONE* 6(9):e24685).

**The largest double-blind trial missed every primary endpoint.** `[VA]`

> "While no effect was found for the primary outcome measures, tinnitus distress, as measured by the Tinnitus Questionnaire, a secondary outcome measure, developed differently in the two groups. The treatment group showed higher distress scores while the placebo group revealed lower distress scores after the training. However, this effect did not reach significance in post-hoc analysis and disappeared at follow-up measurements."

Stein et al. (2016), *BMC Neurol* 16:38, ISRCTN04840953, n = 100 — peer-reviewed. A second RCT (n = 104) concluded only that TMNMT *"is an optional treatment […] However, more research is needed to draw firm conclusions"* `[VA]` (Atipas et al. 2021, *J Int Adv Otol*).

**Three reasons this cannot become an EQ feature.** The evidence is mixed-to-null at the largest sample sizes; the intervention is a months-long listening regimen rather than a static preset, so a notch in a playback chain is not the thing that was tested; and mechanically it **removes** energy, which is the opposite of the compensatory boost users ask for. A user who asks the tuner to notch their tinnitus frequency should get a `discuss` with `basis: "cited"` or `"unverified"` as appropriate, saying the evidence does not support it — not a filter.

### 7.3 Hyperacusis inverts the request

In hyperacusis the discomfort ceiling drops from about 100 dB HL to 70–80 dB HL `[VA]` (Shi et al. 2022, as §3.1), where recruitment leaves it roughly intact. Reported prevalence spans 0.2–17.2 % in the general population, and the source attaches its own caveat that the spread reflects absent standard criteria `[VA]` (Ren et al. 2021, *Front Neurol* 12:706555).

**Our inference, labeled as such and not sourced:** a 20–30 dB reduction in headroom between comfortable listening and discomfort means a broadband or treble boost consumes a large fraction of what remains. A verbatim source stating that boosting is counterproductive in hyperacusis **was sought and not confirmed** (§10) — so this must be presented as reasoning from the quoted UCL figures, never as a source claim. Hyperacusis is also a NICE referral trigger in its own right (§8).

## 8. The medical boundary — recognition, not diagnosis

The tuner is not diagnosing anything. It needs these criteria for one reason: a complaint can arrive that describes a time-critical medical event, and a tuner that silently EQs it has done real harm by omission. The response is a `clarify` plus a `recommends` to seek assessment — **never** a refusal, a disabled control, or an idle gate.

**Sudden sensorineural hearing loss is an emergency with a treatment window.** `[VA]`

> "Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) is an ENT emergency and is defined as a loss of hearing of 30 dB HL or more, over at least 3 contiguous frequencies, that develops within 3 days."

NICE NG98 evidence review, *Sudden sensorineural hearing loss* — standards/guideline body. AAO-HNS guidance offers corticosteroids as initial therapy *"within two weeks of symptom onset"* `[VA]` (AAO-HNS fact sheet for Chandrasekhar et al. 2019). **Time matters here in a way it does not anywhere else in this project.**

Referral triggers, verbatim from NICE NG98 recommendation 1.1.6 `[VA]`:

> "* unilateral or asymmetric hearing loss as a primary concern
> * hearing loss that fluctuates and is not associated with an upper respiratory tract infection
> * hyperacusis (intolerance to everyday sounds that causes significant distress and affects a person's day-to-day activities)
> * persistent tinnitus that is unilateral, pulsatile, has significantly changed in nature or is causing distress
> * vertigo that has not fully resolved or is recurrent
> * hearing loss that is not age related."

NICE, *Hearing loss in adults: assessment and management*, NG98 (2018, updated 2023) — guideline body. Sudden onset within the past 30 days warrants referral *"to be seen within 24 hours"*. NICE NG155 adds imaging for pulsatile tinnitus, and — worth carrying in a tool that talks to distressed users — immediate crisis referral for *"tinnitus associated with a high risk of suicide."* `[VA]`

**Aural fullness is not on NICE's list.** It is frequently repeated as a red flag and was not found as one in any guideline read; do not add it.

The same boundary exists in US regulation, which is evidence it is not our invention. Pre-2022 21 CFR 801.420(c)(2) listed *"History of sudden or rapidly progressive hearing loss within the previous 90 days"* and *"Unilateral hearing loss of sudden or recent onset within the previous 90 days"* among conditions warranting prompt physician consultation `[VA]`. The current OTC rule draws the unilateral/bilateral line explicitly `[VA]`:

> "FDA has included tinnitus in one ear as a condition for which users should seek medical evaluation. FDA is declining to expand upon this labeling requirement to include tinnitus in both ears since bilateral tinnitus often occurs in the presence of any degree of hearing loss. As such, the warning would be overly broad."

87 FR 50698 (17 Aug 2022) — regulatory primary. **Unilateral is the flag; bilateral is not.** That is the asymmetry test stated by a regulator with its reasoning attached, and it is a good model for the tuner's own threshold: one ear, sudden, or changed — say something once. Both ears, longstanding, stable — get on with the tuning.

**Asymmetry is also structurally out of reach**, since all four change types are stereo-symmetric (§4.3). The clinical dB criterion for significant asymmetry was not obtained (§10); what is sourced is that in noise-exposed populations *"the incidence of asymmetrical hearing loss […] varies widely between 4.7 and 36%"* `[VA]` (Le et al. 2017, *J Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 46:41), and that referral to rule out a retrocochlear lesion comes **before** attributing asymmetric loss to noise `[VA]` (ACOEM 2002).

Finally, the sentence a regulator requires amplification devices to carry is a good one for the tuner's own humility `[VA]`: *"A statement that a hearing aid will not restore normal hearing and will not prevent or improve a hearing impairment resulting from organic conditions."* — 21 CFR 801.420(c)(1)(viii).

## 9. Synthesis — reading a complaint that may be hearing loss

The failure mode this document exists to prevent is **confident compensation**: a user mentions hearing loss, the tuner infers a curve from it, and produces a large boost that is wrong in magnitude, wrong in level-dependence, possibly aimed into a dead region, and narrated with a precision nothing underneath it supports.

What the tuner should actually do:

1. **Never infer a curve from an age, a grade, or a self-description.** The 10th-to-90th-percentile spread at one age and frequency is ~54 dB (§1.4), grade schemes disagree (§1.3), and a four-frequency better-ear average discards the shape entirely.
2. **Never treat a threshold shift as a gain figure.** Half is the population approximation, the individual spread around it is ±10 dB at one frequency, and three literatures agree listeners take less than prescribed (§2).
3. **Start well below what the arithmetic suggests, broad, and let the listener drive.** This is `PRIMER.md`'s existing prefer-additive-fills and user-corrects-by-ear discipline, reaching the same place from a second direction.
4. **Prefer the edge of a loss region to its interior** (§3.3). Concrete, sourced, and it changes where a shelf's corner goes.
5. **Keep broad moves broad.** The low-Q ruling gets *stronger* for impaired listeners, though not for the reason `PSYCHOACOUSTICS.md` §1 currently gives (§3.2).
6. **Say what is out of reach, once, without refusing anything.** No compression, no per-ear, no calibrated level (§4.3). Level-dependent problems, asymmetry and diplacusis are `clarify` + `recommends` cases.
7. **Recognize the time-critical pattern.** Sudden, unilateral, or newly changed — one factual sentence pointing at assessment, then continue serving the user (§8).
8. **Do not claim a hearing-loss target curve exists — but do know the one sourced starting magnitude.** Aided listeners preferred essentially the factory tilt; **unaided** listeners preferred a rising tilt of about **+0.6 dB/octave**, roughly 4 dB across 125 Hz–8 kHz, measured within-subjects at `p = 0.002, d = 0.9`. HQPTuner's users are unaided, so that figure is the relevant one — as a gentle starting tilt with evidence behind it, not a curve derived from anyone's audiogram (§5.2). Loss predicts wanting the vocal louder (`r = 0.7`) far better than it predicts wanting more treble (`r = 0.3`, marginal), and the vocal is out of reach.
9. **Note the exposure cost of a large boost once** (§6.3). The exchange rate is 3 dB and +6 dB quarters the permitted week — **but that is broadband A-weighted level, and there is no per-band dose**, so never apply the table to a single band's gain. Never tell a user a setting is safe; the standard itself forbids it for a tool this uncalibrated.

**And the framing that should survive all of it.** A listener who has lost high-frequency hearing is not asking to be fixed; they are asking for the best available compromise, and they are the only one who can hear the result. The tuner's job is to keep the arithmetic honest and the narration modest, not to promise restoration that neither EQ nor a hearing aid delivers.
